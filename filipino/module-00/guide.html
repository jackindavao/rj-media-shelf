Shared-reading guide

Dorothy at ang Pulang Sumbrero · Translation, vocabulary, grammar, questions, and a suggested first week.

Purpose: This is a calibration reader, not a test and not an attempt to imitate RJ's Grade 3 textbook. Its job is to reveal what she can understand with supportive shared reading and repeated exposure.

Open child reader Print this guide

Keep the reading at the center

The governing rule: protect comprehension and enjoyment. RJ may point, act, answer in English, echo a line, or simply listen. Correct only what prevents understanding. Stop while the experience is still positive.

The story has 253 running Filipino words on 12 pages. The repeated frames—Tumingin sila…, Wala…, location phrases, colors, and the missing-hat problem—are intentional. The final pages contain somewhat harder verb forms, but the illustrations and already-known plot carry much of their meaning.

A possible first five readings

SessionSuggested readingWhat matters
1Preview the six words; read pages 1–3 by modeling and echoing.Understand the characters, hat, wind, and problem. Stop there if that fills the session.
2Reread pages 1–3; continue through page 6.Let RJ complete repeated words or lines. Point to the places searched.
3Quickly revisit pages 1–6; read pages 7–9.Enjoy the three looking directions and Toto's discovery.
4Read pages 9–12, then revisit a favorite earlier page.Follow the solution. Do not require an oral retelling.
5+Try the whole story, alternate pages, take character roles, or answer a few questions.Notice increasing ease, anticipation, and independent recognition.

This schedule is only a starting point. A single page can be a successful session. Conversely, if RJ wants to continue, there is no instructional reason to stop at the suggested boundary.

Pronunciation support

  • Filipino vowels are usually steady: a as in father, e as in bed, i as in machine, o as in go, and u as in rule.
  • ng is the final sound in English sing; Filipino can also begin a syllable with it.
  • Ordinary spelling does not show every stress distinction. A natural model from RJ's aunt or another fluent speaker is more useful than a complicated written stress system at this stage.
  • If possible, ask a fluent speaker to read or record the story once. Do not let uncertainty about perfect pronunciation prevent a warm shared reading.

Grammar that matters in this story

1. Filipino markers do work that English word order often does

Si introduces a person's name in the central role: Masaya si Dorothy. Ang does similar work for ordinary nouns: Masaya ang ibon. Ni and ng mark other roles and possession: kamay ni Tik-Tok, sa ilalim ng puno. At this stage, recognize these as small guide words; do not ask RJ to memorize a case system.

2. May and nasa are different

May maliit na ibon introduces or asserts that a bird exists: “There is a small bird.” Nasa hardin si Dorothy locates an already identified Dorothy: “Dorothy is in the garden.”

3. Adjectives connect with na or -ng

The same linker appears in two shapes: maliit na ibon but pulang sumbrero, magandang araw, and malaking bato. The attached -ng is not a new meaning word; it connects the description to the noun.

4. One root can appear in several verb forms

Filipino verbs change internally and with affixes. The story deliberately lets context and pictures introduce a few forms: hanap → naghanap / hahanapin / hinahanap; tingin → tumingin; tulong → tutulong; tawa → tumawa. The useful lesson for now is simply that these related-looking words are related—not that RJ should produce a conjugation table.

5. Pronouns often come after the verb

Tumingin sila literally follows the order “looked they.” Tumingin silang lahat joins sila to lahat with -ng: “They all looked.”

6. Possessive pronouns follow the noun

sumbrero ko is “my hat,” while kamay niya is “his/her hand.” Context identifies whether niya means his, her, or its.

What to observe before Module 1

After several readings, jot down informal observations rather than a score:

  • Which page first became easy or predictable?
  • Can RJ follow the main problem and solution without the English translation?
  • Which repeated words does she recognize in a new place?
  • Does she understand location phrases such as sa ilalim, sa tabi, and sa taas?
  • Does echo reading, reading together, or alternating lines work best?
  • At what point does attention or enjoyment begin to fall?
Useful report for the next module: “We read it four times. Pages 1–5 became comfortable; pages 7 and 11 were hard. She understood the plot and answered four questions, but needed help with niya and the verb forms.” That is enough information to adjust vocabulary, sentence length, repetition, and morphology.
