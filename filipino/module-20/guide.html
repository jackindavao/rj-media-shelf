Dorothy, Trot, at ang Parisukat na Buhol

Dorothy meets Trot and Captain Bill, then the two girls learn one concrete making skill while preparing a picnic bundle for a trip across the Lake of Lilies.

Two repairs to Module 19's questions

Your objections were well founded. Nag-atubili means “hesitated,” but the story explicitly supplied fear, not uncertain indecision. It was therefore a mismatch as well as unpreviewed vocabulary. The question has been replaced by the concrete Bakit huminto muna ang dalawang ibon sa bakod?

Sapat can mean “enough” or “sufficient,” but Bakit hindi sapat na makita…? introduced a new abstract predicate and left “enough for what?” implicit. The revised question says exactly what page 8 shows: Bakit hindi pa nila natagpuan ang paanyaya nang makita ni Dorothy ang lasong bughaw? Future end questions will similarly favor story language unless a new question word is deliberately previewed.

Why this story

After the cornfield search, this is a small making story rather than a concept-heavy science lesson. It honors RJ's request for Trot and Captain Bill, gives Dorothy a new violet dress, and adds one useful physical sequence that can be understood directly from the action: the first crossing goes one way and the second reverses it.

The square knot is presented only as a binding knot for a light bundle. It is not presented as a safety, rescue, climbing, lifting, or load-bearing knot. Page 9 makes that boundary part of the story.

English translation

Page 1

Dorothy put on a new violet dress. With the two birds, she walked to the Lake of Lilies.

Page 2

There she met Trot, a girl her age, and Captain Bill, a sailor with a wooden leg. They had a boat.

Page 3

They were going to have a picnic on the small island. Trot put the basket and rolled blanket in the boat. But the blanket opened and covered the basket.

Page 4

Trot wrapped the blanket with red rope. Twice she crossed the right end over the left. When she lifted it, the knot twisted and came loose.

Page 5

“There is a proper way,” said Captain Bill. “The square knot is flat and a sturdy tie for a bundle.”

Page 6

Trot held the two ends. She put the right over the left. Then she passed it underneath and pulled.

Page 7

“Let us reverse it,” said Dorothy. She put the left over the right. She passed it underneath and pulled tightly.

Page 8

The knot became flat. The children pulled the opposite ends. The knot did not twist or come loose. The blanket was tied securely.

Page 9

“This knot is for a bundle,” said Captain Bill. “Do not use it to tie or lift a person.”

Page 10

They put the bundle and basket in the boat. The children got aboard. Captain Bill rowed while the bird friends perched at the front of the boat.

Page 11

Upon reaching the island, Trot untied the knot. It loosened. Dorothy and Trot spread the blanket beneath the flowering tree.

Page 12

They ate beside the lake. Captain Bill told stories about the sea. Dorothy and Trot planned their next adventure while the birds listened.

Adult source and safety note

The rope drawings are story illustrations, not accurate knot diagrams. In particular, they do not reliably show the rope paths or distinguish a square knot from a granny knot. The words “right over left, then left over right” are a mnemonic for two complete overhand crossings, not stand-alone instructions. A real piece of cord and an adult demonstration are better for comparing these knots. Page 9 sets the rule for this activity: tie the blanket, not a person. It is not a claim about what a cord can physically do.

Trot and Captain Bill come from L. Frank Baum's public-domain Oz-related books, including The Sea Fairies, Sky Island, and The Scarecrow of Oz. Their visual treatment here is original and avoids film-specific likenesses.

The tying order follows the standard square or reef knot: one crossing, then the opposite crossing. The Bureau of Fire Protection's young-adult fire-safety material gives the familiar right-over-left, then left-over-right sequence. For the limitation that matters here, NOAA's knot guide describes the reef knot as a binding knot and warns against using it to join ropes in safety-critical situations. The story therefore uses it only on a picnic bundle and explicitly excludes tying or lifting a person.
