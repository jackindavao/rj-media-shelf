Why Polychrome stays

RJ noticed correctly that Polychrome did not leave at the end of Module 6. Module 7 begins the next morning with nanatili si Polychrome (“Polychrome remained”) and ends with her promise, Mananatili muna ako kasama ninyo (“I will stay with you for a while”). She is part of the work throughout rather than appearing only for a decorative entrance.

The painting plot also answers RJ's own creative response to the character. Polychrome brings knowledge of color, but Dorothy and Tik-Tok perform two of the mixtures and everyone contributes to the finished gift for Ozma.

The small art-and-science idea

For traditional children's paint mixing, red, yellow, and blue are treated as primary colors. Red plus yellow makes orange, yellow plus blue makes green, and red plus blue makes purple or violet. This is the familiar pigment model used in elementary art activities; mixing colored light follows a different model.

The story's three mixtures follow guidance from the Smithsonian Hirshhorn's “Red, Yellow, Blue” mini art lesson and the National Gallery of Art's “Elements of Art: Color”. Actual paint results vary with pigment and proportion, so a real experiment may produce different shades while preserving the same basic relationships.

Story map

  1. Polychrome remains in the garden and finds Dorothy preparing a picture for Ozma.
  2. Only red, yellow, and blue paint are on the table; the bird wants an orange flower.
  3. Polychrome proposes mixing pairs of colors in three separate bowls.
  4. Dorothy mixes red and yellow to make orange.
  5. Tik-Tok mixes yellow and blue to make green.
  6. Polychrome mixes red and blue to make violet.
  7. They finish the picture, give it to Ozma, and Polychrome promises to stay.

How to handle an unexpected answer

  1. Check meaning, not grammar. If RJ's English, Filipino, or pointing identifies the right person, number, or colors, accept it.
  2. If the meaning differs, go to the evidence page. Read only the relevant sentence and look at its picture.
  3. Offer the printed choice. For example, “Pula at dilaw, or dilaw at bughaw?” No new Filipino is needed.
  4. Read the short answer once. Then move on. There is no need to explain why an inflection is wrong or ask RJ to repeat a correction.

A response that remains different after rereading is useful observation for the next module, not a failure that must be repaired immediately.

Pronunciation key

The key and fluent recording remain the right combination. A practical English reading of Polychrome is POL-ee-krohm; following the nephew's consistent pronunciation is more important than reproducing a particular accent.

Quick key for this story: laráwan, pintúra, garapón, mangkók, hahaluín, hinálo, ipinintá, paruparó, mananatíli. These are adult-only cues; keep the marks out of RJ's reader.

For connected rhythm and any uncertain stress, continue to prefer the fluent recording over isolated rules.

Grammar and patterns that matter

1. A planned action becomes a completed action

Hahaluin natin ang dalawang kulay announces the plan. Each later hinalo niya reports the mixing as it happens. The three repeated frames make the relationship visible without a formal lesson.

2. Naging states each result

Naging kahel, naging berde, and naging lila use exactly the same frame. The changed paint in each illustration supplies the meaning.

3. Nanatili becomes mananatili

The opening says Polychrome remained overnight; her final line says she will remain. The meaning and timing are available from the story even if RJ does not describe the morphology explicitly.

4. Evidence is more useful than grammar correction

The questions ask for a recipient, number, color pair, or yes/no meaning. A phrase such as pula at dilaw is a complete communicative answer. If the meaning differs, reread the evidence rather than explaining sentence construction.

This is an original educational story using characters from L. Frank Baum's literary Oz world.
