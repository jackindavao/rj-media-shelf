For the adult reading partner

Dorothy at si Propesor Wogglebug at ang Munting Telepono

This guide lets a non-Filipino-speaking adult support the story accurately without turning shared reading into a grammar lesson or a translation test.

Calibration decision

Module 8 produced excellent comprehension, a practical count of 28 unfamiliar forms, more voluntary Filipino answers, and sustained enjoyment. That is evidence to hold the reading level steady. This module therefore keeps the same 12-page scale, compact science cluster, causal test sequence, and returning cast. The requested question bank expands from three to four choices per page; it is still a menu, never a 48-question assignment.

At a glance

  • 12 story pages and exactly 265 running Filipino words
  • Seven original narrative illustrations
  • Six end-of-story questions
  • 48 natural page questions with exact short answers—four per page
  • 30 programmatically new surface forms beyond the TSV and Modules 0–8
  • One experiment: loose string, obstructed string, then taut free string
  • Contextual links among narinig, nayanig, and panginginig

Why this experiment fits

Professor Wogglebug returns as a guide who proposes, observes, and revises rather than simply announcing an answer. A cup-and-string telephone gives the story three visible conditions to compare. It also connects naturally with the earlier listening story and with the star book from Module 8.

The small science idea is accurate: speaking makes the bottom of the first cup vibrate; a taut string that can vibrate freely carries that motion to the second cup. A loose string loses much of the motion, while touching or pinching the string interferes with it. The adult background references are the Exploratorium's cup-telephone activity and Science World's telephone-lines activity. The story uses only the part RJ can see and test.

Story map

  1. Pages 1–3: Wogglebug returns, Dorothy wants to call Ozma, and the professor makes a two-cup telephone.
  2. Pages 4–6: Loose string fails; taut string touching a branch produces a broken message.
  3. Pages 7–8: Taut, unobstructed string carries a clear message.
  4. Pages 9–11: Polychrome routes the string through the open gate and Ozma hears the invitation.
  5. Page 12: Ozma joins the group under the stars and Wogglebug promises another experiment.

Your line-and-meaning routine

The routine you described is doing important work. Alternating Filipino lines and asking for the idea in English checks whether the words are becoming a scene in RJ's mind, not merely a pronounceable sound stream. A natural paraphrase is better evidence than a memorized word-for-word translation, so keep accepting answers that preserve the event, relationship, and important details.

There is no need to stop the routine. On familiar material, gently lengthen the amount she holds before the meaning check:

  • For a new or difficult page, keep the present line-by-line exchange.
  • For one familiar page, read two or three lines before asking, “What picture did that make?”
  • Occasionally let a whole familiar page flow, then ask for one short retelling.
  • Sometimes replace English with pointing, acting, choosing between two pictures, or predicting the next action.
  • Once in a session, read a short stretch without interruption and discuss it afterward, so comprehension also learns to persist across sentences.

Rereading one complete older module is valuable while it remains enjoyable. It can be treated as fluent pleasure reading: fewer pauses, one retelling at the end, and no need to question every page. If the total session ever begins to feel long, preserve the new story and rotate the older reread rather than increasing effort.

Using the page-by-page questions

Choose one question after a page and save the other three for later rereadings. A short phrase is a complete success. If RJ answers in English, that is full evidence of comprehension; you may read the printed Filipino answer once without asking her to repeat it.

If an answer differs in meaning, reread the page, offer a simple choice drawn from the wording, read the model once, and move on. This keeps the correction tied to evidence and avoids improvised grammar explanations.

English translation

Page 1

That afternoon, Professor Wogglebug returned to the garden. He was carrying two paper cups, a long string, and a small box.

Page 2

Ozma was on the other side of the hedge. Dorothy wanted to call her, but her quiet voice did not reach.

Page 3

“We will make a little telephone,” said the professor. He made a hole in each cup. He passed the string through the holes and tied the ends.

Page 4

Dorothy and Polychrome held the cups. The string was loose and lay on the grass. Dorothy spoke, but Polychrome heard nothing.

Page 5

“The string must be tight,” explained the professor. Polychrome moved away until it was tight. But the string was touching a low branch.

Page 6

Dorothy spoke again. What Polychrome heard was broken up. They looked at the string. “It must not touch anything,” said Wogglebug.

Page 7

They moved to the path. The string was tight, and it touched nothing. Dorothy spoke. The bottom of the cup vibrated a little.

Page 8

The string carried the vibration to the other cup. Polychrome brought it to her ear. She clearly heard, “The book about the stars is on the table!”

Page 9

Polychrome carried one cup to Ozma. They carefully stretched the string through the open gate. It did not touch the hedge.

Page 10

Dorothy spoke into her cup. “Ozma, we are going to read about the stars!” Ozma listened. She heard the whole invitation.

Page 11

“I will come!” Ozma answered. “The string is tight, nothing is touching it, and the cup is close to my ear. That is why your voice is clear.”

Page 12

Ozma arrived carrying the book. Professor Wogglebug bowed. “Next time, another experiment!” he said. They sat beneath the stars.

Grammar and patterns that matter

1. Narinig marks what was heard

Walang narinig si Polychrome means “Polychrome heard nothing,” while malinaw niyang narinig means she heard it clearly. The changing experimental conditions carry the meaning; no conjugation lesson is needed.

2. The word family makes motion visible

Nayanig describes the cup bottom vibrating; panginginig names the vibration carried by the string. Recognizing the shared sound and shared event is enough.

3. Familiar dikit transfers into a new experiment

Module 8 used dumidikit and dumikit with a magnet. Here nakadikit, dumikit, and dumidikit describe unwanted contact with the string. This is productive transfer, not a vocabulary reset.

4. The answer can be shorter than the question

Sa landas, kay Ozma, wala, and dalawa are complete conversational answers. They give RJ usable Filipino without requiring a full sentence every time.

What to observe before Module 10

Continue with informal observations rather than a score:

  • Can RJ explain separately why the loose string and the branch-contact string fail?
  • Can she name the two successful conditions: taut string and nothing touching it?
  • Does she understand that the voice vibrates the cup and the string carries the vibration?
  • Does familiar dumikit/dumidikit transfer naturally into nakadikit here?
  • Can she retell how Polychrome routes the telephone to Ozma?
  • Are four page-question choices useful, or does any pattern sound unnatural when read aloud?
  • Does she answer more often in Filipino, and which short answer patterns become reusable?
  • On a familiar page, can the meaning check move from every line to two or three lines without losing the mental picture?
  • Does the complete older-module reread remain pleasurable, or does it ever make the session too long?
  • What unfamiliar-word count results from the same practical method?
  • Do all essential string positions agree with the prose, and which harmless differences invite useful close reading?
  • Do pronunciation support, the four-day rhythm, and enjoyment remain strong, and which illustration becomes her favorite?
