For the adult reading with RJ

Dorothy at ang Saranggolang Dilaw

This guide keeps explanation, translation, and pronunciation support away from the child story. Use only as much as helps the shared reading remain confident and enjoyable.

Calibration decision

Module 3 remained completely clear and highly enjoyable. RJ understood the rejected waterfall, sound sequence, patient pause, and solution; the illustrations needed no explanation; the pronunciation key and fluent recording both helped; and the four-day cumulative rhythm still fit.

The important new evidence is vocabulary. Counting different inflections separately, the adult noticed roughly 50 unfamiliar surface forms. That was manageable, but it is a ceiling rather than a new target. Module 4 therefore uses a smaller, tightly pictured kite vocabulary and deliberately reuses familiar language about building, locations, wind, waiting, safety, and trying again. Its 34 surface forms absent from both the TSV and earlier stories include many ordinary variants or transparent members of familiar families, so the likely genuinely new conceptual load remains much smaller.

Story map

  1. Dorothy proposes making a kite, and the friends build a yellow one together.
  2. They first try beneath the trees, where the tail catches on a low branch.
  3. They next try beside the river, where the wind is dangerously strong.
  4. They wait until the wind becomes gentler.
  5. They choose an open hill, safely away from low branches and water.
  6. The kite rises; when it dips, Dorothy pulls the string and it climbs again.

English translation

PageEnglish
1One morning, Dorothy brought a large sheet of paper into the garden. “We are going to make a kite,” she said. The friends came over at once.
2They made a yellow kite. Dorothy placed two sticks on the paper. Tik-Tok tied the middle. The bird helped with the tail.
3Soon the kite was ready. “Will it fly high?” the bird asked. “We will try it,” Dorothy answered.
4They went beneath the trees. Dorothy ran while holding the string. The kite rose, but its tail caught on a low branch.
5Tik-Tok carefully freed the tail. “There are many branches here,” he said. “We need an open place.” They searched.
6They went beside the river. There were no branches there. But suddenly the wind became strong. It pulled the kite. Dorothy nearly let go of the string.
7Tik-Tok held the string. “The wind is too strong here,” Dorothy said. “It isn't safe here beside the water.” They went back.
8They waited. After a while, the wind grew weaker. “The wind is gentle now,” the bird said. “Let's go to the hill!”
9The top of the hill was open. There were no low branches, and it was far from the river. Dorothy held the string. Toto ran beside her.
10The kite slowly rose. It flew above their heads. The bird clapped. Tik-Tok danced.
11At one point, the kite went down. Dorothy pulled the string. The kite flew again. “The right place and the right wind!” she said.
12The yellow kite rose into the sky. It did not get caught, and the friends were safe. Beneath it, they all laughed while the wind blew.

Grammar that matters in this story

1. RJ's pattern observation is valuable—and provisional

RJ noticed that forms containing -um- often referred to completed events, while an early repeated syllable often pointed forward. That is excellent pattern-finding in these particular sentences: gagawa → gumawa and lilipad → lumipad behave that way here. There is no need to teach a rule yet.

The careful adult version is that Filipino verbs mark aspect—how an action unfolds or is viewed—more centrally than simple English-style tense. -um- does not always mean “past,” and reduplication does not always mean “future.” Context, affixes, and the verb family work together. Keep welcoming the observation while treating it as a hypothesis that later stories will refine.

2. A plan becomes a completed action

Gagawa tayo ng saranggola announces the plan. Gumawa sila ng saranggolang dilaw reports the action. The finished-kite picture makes the shift concrete.

3. A prediction becomes an observed result

Lilipad ba ito? asks what the kite will do. Later, lumipad ito reports what the friends see. RJ can understand that contrast without naming its morphology.

4. Changes in degree share a visible pattern

Lumakas ang hangin and humina ang hangin describe opposite changes. The river gust and later calm scene carry the meaning. Likewise, umangat, bumaba, and tumaas describe the kite's changing position.

5. The location choice uses two kinds of evidence

The hill is suitable because of what is absent—no low branches or nearby water—and what is present—open space and gentle wind. That supports causal reasoning while the prose remains simple.
