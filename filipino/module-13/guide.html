For the adult reading partner

Dorothy at si Button-Bright sa Apat na Landas

This guide lets a non-Filipino-speaking adult support the story accurately without turning shared reading into a grammar lesson or a translation test.

Calibration decision

Module 12 again produced complete narrative and scientific understanding, contextual transfer, growing confidence with Filipino answers, and strong enjoyment. The practical unfamiliar count was 35, including words in the page questions. That wider count is useful: the oral question bank is part of the language RJ meets, so Module 13 keeps both the story and its questions lexically conservative. The child story remains at 12 pages and exactly 265 words, with 15 programmatically new story surface forms beyond the TSV and Modules 0–12. The 48-question bank and its model answers add 13 more first-appearance forms, for 28 across the Filipino reading-and-question package.

RJ's easiest launch points remain sino, static nasaan, and concrete ano. Each page therefore includes at least one quick retrieval question and one optional question carrying an action, sequence, direction, or cause. Quoting the relevant story phrase is a fully successful Filipino answer.

Why the compass sequence works

A magnetic compass needle turns freely and aligns roughly north–south. On the story's compass, the red end is the north-pointing end. Laying the compass flat lets the needle move freely. Turning the map until its north arrow agrees with the compass puts the map and the visible paths in the same orientation; on that oriented map, east is to the right of north.

The child story says simply that the red end points north. More precisely, it points toward magnetic north, which can differ from geographic north by an angle called magnetic declination. That distinction matters in precise real-world navigation, but not for the small fictional garden route. The U.S. National Park Service's elementary Compass Course likewise begins by holding a compass level, identifying the red north end, and aligning directions; the U.S. Geological Survey explains the four cardinal directions in Finding Your Way With Map and Compass.

For a real outdoor activity, keep a compass away from magnets and large metal objects, which can disturb the needle. Use a familiar supervised area; this story is not an invitation for a child to navigate alone.

Story map

  1. Pages 1–3: Dorothy, Toto, and the yellow bird find Button-Bright lost among four similar-looking paths.
  2. Pages 4–6: A palace symbol and north arrow are on his map. Dorothy lays the compass flat; Button-Bright turns the map until its north arrow and the red needle agree.
  3. Pages 7–8: Button-Bright identifies the palace east of them and leads the group onto the eastern path.
  4. Pages 9–10: At another fork, he prevents a wrong turn by repeating the check himself.
  5. Pages 11–12: They reach the green palace, where Button-Bright explains the different jobs of map and compass.

Thirty-minute reading rhythm

Continue translating all words when a page is genuinely new if that makes the first encounter secure. On familiar pages, the preferred two- or three-line meaning check can preserve the mental picture with fewer interruptions. One targeted question can then recover a detail. It is not necessary to prove every word on every rereading.

For Filipino production, begin with a question likely to produce a quick phrase, then offer one harder direction or cause question only if attention remains good. English, a mixed-language answer, pointing to the map, or quoting the story all demonstrate comprehension.

Random-page review game

The numbered-card idea is excellent for an older, already familiar module. It changes rereading from serial recall into retrieval: RJ must rebuild the page's scene without relying on what came immediately before. It also preserves play and choice.

  1. Use the cards only after the story has first been learned in order.
  2. Draw one number, read that page, and choose one of its four printed questions.
  3. If the page feels easy, ask what happened immediately before or after it; English is fine.
  4. Put the card in a finished pile so all 12 pages eventually appear once.
  5. If context is genuinely missing, peek at the neighboring page rather than turning the moment into a memory test.

For safe improvisation, reuse a printed frame and change only one known element: Sino ang…?, Nasaan ang…?, Ano ang…?, or Ilan ang…?. The printed bank remains the default; there is no need to invent new verb forms.

End-of-story answer bank

1. Bakit naligaw si Button-Bright?

Shortest: Apat ang landas.

Model: Hindi niya alam kung saan liliko dahil pareho ang mga puno.

Evidence: pages 1–3. Choice: Magkakaiba ba o pareho ang mga puno?

2. Ano ang ginawa ng karayom?

Shortest: Umiikot, saka tumigil.

Model: Umiikot ang karayom at tumigil na nakaturo sa hilaga.

Evidence: page 5. Choice: Sa hilaga ba o silangan nakaturo ang pulang dulo?

3. Paano itinapat ang mapa?

Shortest: Palaso sa pulang karayom.

Model: Itinapat niya ang palaso ng hilaga sa pulang karayom.

Evidence: pages 4–6. Choice: Iniikot ba ang mapa o ang bato?

4. Paano nila pinili ang silangan?

Shortest: Nasa silangan ang bituin.

Model: Nasa kanan ng hilaga ang bituin ng palasyo, kaya pinili nila ang silangan.

Evidence: pages 6–8. Choice: Nasa silangan ba o hilaga ang palasyo?

5. Ano ang ginawa sa ikalawang sangandaan?

Shortest: Ginamit muli ang kompas.

Model: Inilagay ni Button-Bright ang kompas nang patag at itinapat muli ang mapa.

Evidence: pages 9–10. Choice: Lumiko ba agad o nagsuri muna?

6. Ano ang naitulong ng bawat gamit?

Shortest: Lugar at direksiyon.

Model: Ipinakita ng mapa ang lugar at ipinakita ng kompas ang direksiyon.

Evidence: pages 11–12. Choice: Alin ang nagpakita ng direksiyon—mapa o kompas?

English translation

Page 1

One morning, Dorothy and Toto walked in the garden. The little yellow bird was with them. At the end of the road, a child was beside four paths.

Page 2

“I am Button-Bright,” said the child. “I am looking for the palace, but I got lost.” He was holding a small map and compass.

Page 3

“Which path did you take?” Dorothy asked. Button-Bright shook his head. “I do not know where to turn. The trees look alike.”

Page 4

He opened the map. There was a star for the palace and an arrow for north. But the map was still facing the wrong direction.

Page 5

Dorothy placed the compass flat on a stone. The needle turned. When it stopped, the red end pointed north.

Page 6

Button-Bright turned the map. He lined up its north arrow with the red needle. “The map is facing the right direction now!”

Page 7

“Where is the palace?” Dorothy asked. Button-Bright showed the star. It was east, to the right of north. “The road is east!”

Page 8

Button-Bright walked first. They turned onto the eastern path. Dorothy and Toto followed. The bird flew above.

Page 9

At another fork, there were two paths. They almost turned the wrong way. “Let us use the compass again,” said Button-Bright.

Page 10

He placed the compass flat. The needle turned and pointed north. He aligned the map. “This is still the road to the palace.”

Page 11

They followed the path until the green palace appeared behind the trees. “We did not go in circles this time!” said Button-Bright.

Page 12

Ozma and Polychrome welcomed them. The bird sang on Button-Bright's shoulder. “The map showed the place. The compass showed the direction,” he explained.

Let the route carry the forms

Saan liliko? expresses the still-open choice. Lumiko sila reports the choice they made. Umiikot describes the needle in motion, while familiar umikot describes going around. The map, movement, and pictures provide the contrast; no conjugation lesson is needed.

Why the shortest answers work

Si Button-Bright, sa hilaga, ang mapa, and dalawang landas are natural complete answers. When RJ quotes one from the page, she is selecting the grammatical form the question calls for—not avoiding the task.

Production note

This is an original educational story inspired by the public-domain literary world of Oz. Button-Bright's recurring design is an original cheerful young traveler, slightly shorter than Dorothy, with tousled sandy-blond hair, a cream sailor blouse with a navy collar and bright brass buttons, navy knee-length shorts, cream socks, brown boots, and a small round sailor cap. The little yellow bird in the red floppy hat is an original recurring friend created for this project rather than a claimed Baum character. The prose, navigation sequence, questions, and watercolor-and-colored-pencil illustrations were created for this reading project and avoid film-specific likenesses and embedded text.
