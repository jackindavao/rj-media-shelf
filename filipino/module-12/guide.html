For the adult reading partner

Dorothy at ang Tubig na Umakyat

This guide lets a non-Filipino-speaking adult support the story accurately without turning shared reading into a grammar lesson or a translation test.

Calibration decision

Module 11 produced complete narrative and scientific understanding, reliable transfer among the float and sink forms, successful delayed meaning checks, and continued delight. The practical count rose to about 45 because it includes older words that are not yet secure. This is a reason to keep the level steady and lighten first appearances—not to shorten a happy routine that has missed only one or two days since Module 0. Module 12 therefore holds the scale at 12 pages and 261 words while using only 23 programmatically new surface forms.

The question bank also responds to a useful distinction in RJ's answers: sino is usually easiest; a static position such as “Where is the bird?” is easier than a question that combines an action, object, and location such as “Where did she press the clay?”; and a concrete ano question with one subject is easier than one carrying a full action sequence. Each page retains four choices, but quick questions lead into harder ones rather than all being treated as equal tests.

Why the color reaches the petals

A cut flower continues taking up water through narrow water-conducting tissue in its stem. Colored water makes that otherwise hidden route visible in the stem and petals. Adhesion, cohesion, capillary action, and water loss from the flower all contribute to the upward movement; “the water travels through tiny tubes in the stem” is the accurate child-sized explanation used in the story.

The clear-water flower is important evidence: it receives water under the same conditions but does not acquire red or blue markings. The comparison supports the conclusion that the new petal color came from the colored water rather than from Polychrome painting the flower. The sequence follows Science World's white-carnation demonstration and the U.S. Geological Survey's explanation of capillary action and water movement in plants.

If the family ever repeats the demonstration, use food coloring rather than paint. A fresh diagonal stem cut helps water enter, but an adult should handle any cutting tool. Visible color may appear after several hours and may become stronger overnight; the story's morning-to-afternoon wait is plausible, not instantaneous magic.

Story map

  1. Pages 1–3: Polychrome and the yellow bird return; Tin Woodman arrives with three drooping white flowers; the friends decide to make the water's path visible.
  2. Pages 4–6: They prepare equal clear, red, and blue water, place one flower in each glass, predict, and discover that a few minutes is not enough.
  3. Pages 7–8: Afternoon brings lower water, colored stem streaks, and three clearly different petal outcomes.
  4. Pages 9–10: The friends rule out painting and connect the color with water moving through tiny stem tubes.
  5. Pages 11–12: They show the evidence to Ozma; Dorothy states the result; the bird sings from Tin Woodman's shoulder; Polychrome stays.

Thirty-minute reading rhythm

Continue the two- or three-line meaning check on familiar pages. It is not necessary to verify every word on every reading. If RJ preserves the main mental picture, choose one question to recover an important detail and keep moving; cumulative rereading can fill smaller gaps over time.

For Filipino production, try this order when it feels natural: one sino question, then a static nasaan or concrete ano question, then an optional action, sequence, or cause question. Hesitation on an action-location question does not show that the location concept is missing—the question itself asks her to hold more relationships at once. A one-word or short-phrase answer remains complete success.

End-of-story answer bank

1. Ano ang problema sa mga bulaklak?

Shortest: Nakayuko at malambot.

Model: Nakayuko ang mga bulaklak at malambot ang mga tangkay.

Evidence: pages 2–3. Choice: Kailangan ba nila ng tubig o bato?

2. Paano inihanda ang tatlong baso?

Shortest: Malinaw, pula, at asul.

Model: Pantay ang tubig; malinaw ang una, pula ang ikalawa, at asul ang ikatlo.

Evidence: page 4. Choice: Pantay ba o magkakaiba ang dami ng tubig?

3. Bakit kailangan nilang maghintay?

Shortest: Puti pa ang talulot.

Model: Makalipas ang ilang minuto, puti pa rin ang mga talulot.

Evidence: pages 5–6. Choice: Nagbago ba agad o hindi pa?

4. Ano ang nagbago?

Shortest: Ang mga guhit at gilid.

Model: Lumitaw ang pulang at asul na guhit sa mga tangkay at nagkulay ang mga gilid ng talulot.

Evidence: pages 7–8. Choice: Alin ang nanatiling puti—una o ikatlo?

5. Paano nila nalaman na galing sa tubig ang kulay?

Shortest: Hindi ipininta ang talulot.

Model: Hindi ipininta ang mga talulot, at tumugma ang mga guhit sa kulay ng tubig.

Evidence: pages 7–10. Choice: Mula ba sa pintura o sa tubig?

6. Ano ang natutuhan nila?

Shortest: Umaakyat ang tubig.

Model: Umaakyat ang tubig sa maliliit na tubo ng tangkay hanggang sa talulot.

Evidence: pages 9–12. Choice: Sa tangkay ba umakyat ang tubig?

English translation

Page 1

One morning, Dorothy heard the little yellow bird's song. Polychrome had returned with it. Toto ran toward them.

Page 2

Tin Woodman arrived. He was carrying three white flowers for Ozma. The flowers were drooping and their stems were limp.

Page 3

“They need water,” said Tin Woodman. “But where does the water go?” “Let us color the water so we can see its path,” said Polychrome.

Page 4

The water was level in three clear glasses. The first was colorless. Polychrome made the second red and the third blue.

Page 5

Tin Woodman placed one white flower in each glass. The bird looked at the petals. “Will the color climb?” Dorothy asked.

Page 6

After a few minutes, all the petals were still white. “Not yet,” said Polychrome. “We need to wait.”

Page 7

They returned in the afternoon. There was less water now. There was a red streak and a blue streak in two stems.

Page 8

The edge of the second flower became reddish, and the edge of the third became blue. The first flower remained white.

Page 9

The bird flew above the glasses. The petals had not been painted. The color came from the water.

Page 10

Tin Woodman raised the flower. Dorothy saw small lines in the petal. “The water is climbing through tiny tubes in the stem,” Polychrome explained.

Page 11

They brought the three flowers to Ozma. Dorothy showed the clear, red, and blue water and the petals.

Page 12

“The water climbed through the stem,” Dorothy said. The bird sang on Tin Woodman's shoulder. Polychrome stayed with the friends.

Let the experiment carry the forms

Aakyat kaya? makes a prediction before evidence appears. Umakyat reports what the water did. Umaakyat names the process the group can now explain. The repeated scene supplies the timing; no conjugation exercise is needed.

Why the shortest answers work

Si Polychrome, sa bawat baso, tatlong bulaklak, and sa talulot are natural complete answers. They let RJ practice the exact relationship asked about without needing to rebuild the entire sentence.

Production note

This is an original educational story inspired by the public-domain literary world of Oz. Tin Woodman's new design uses an original friendly silver construction with articulated plates, rivets, a funnel cap, and a heart panel. The prose, experiment sequence, page questions, and watercolor-and-colored-pencil illustrations were created for this reading project. The pictures avoid film-specific likenesses and contain no embedded text.
